2. Unit Overview & Learning Intentions

🔍 What this unit does not do, and why

Most teaching resources list what they cover. This lists what they do not, because a limit you know about is something you can plan around and a limit you discover in week three is a problem. Three of these are deliberate scope decisions; one is genuinely undone; one is not something any document can supply.

What is missingWhat that means for you Why it is like that
Most of the HPE curriculum This unit addresses 10 of the 40 Victorian content descriptions. It services Safety substantially and contributes to three other focus areas. It does not touch the other eight. Deliberate. It is a water safety unit, not a program. The Curriculum Coverage Map lists every code it does not address so your planning can place them elsewhere.
Evidence of student achievement The curriculum codes record what was taught. They are not, by themselves, evidence that students achieved anything. Deliberate. Victorian Curriculum 2.0 reports against achievement standards. Evidence comes from the rubrics, work samples and your moderation — not from a coverage map.
Tiered intervention The scaffolded and simplified versions are Tier 1 differentiation. They are not a Tier 2 or 3 intervention program and should not be counted as one. Deliberate. Intervention is designed around a student, by your school, on data this unit does not hold.
Flexible grouping on evidence The lessons use whole-class and fixed-pair structures. They do not tell you to form and re-form groups during a lesson based on what you are seeing. Genuinely open, and the one gap left. VTLM 2.0 asks for groups formed on evidence of learning. That decision depends on what is in front of you, so the materials prompt it (see the checks-for-understanding repertoire) but cannot script it. Worth adding to your own lesson notes.
Everything that happens in the room Classroom rules and routines, active supervision, how you respond to a student in the moment, the relationships you build with students and families. Not a document’s job. A resource can prompt these; it cannot evidence them. An alignment document that claimed otherwise would be worth less than this one.
Why this section exists at all. This unit was audited in October 2026 against all 14 teaching strategies of the Victorian Teaching and Learning Model 2.0. Twenty technique-level gaps were found; nineteen were closed, and the one that was not is in the table above. The full audit, including what was changed and what the model asks that a resource suite structurally cannot deliver, is in the VTLM 2.0 Alignment Guide. If you are documenting VTLM implementation for an Annual Implementation Plan, read that rather than this summary.

3. VTLM 2.0 Teaching Techniques for This Unit

The Victorian Teaching and Learning Model 2.0 names specific techniques within each of its 14 teaching strategies. This section supplies them for this unit — the actual scripts, tables and schedules, not a restatement of the model. Each block is tagged with the strategy it serves so you can cite it in an Annual Implementation Plan or a VTLM reflection session.

3.1 Advance organiser — the unit in one pictureFOCUS THE LEARNING · P2

Display this at the start of Lesson 1, then return to it at the start of every lesson and point to where the class now is. An advance organiser is not a summary: it is shown before the content, so students can hang new knowledge on a structure they already hold.

LessonThe question it answers What it connects to
1. The problemWho drowns in Victoria, and why them? Students’ own local waterways, named in the opening hook. Nothing precedes this lesson.
2. Reading the waterHow do I tell safe water from dangerous water? Builds on L1’s “where” — the coastal share of deaths becomes a reason to read a beach.
3. When it goes wrongWhat do I actually do? Needs L2: you cannot decide on a rescue without knowing what a rip is doing to the person.
4. Inland and alcoholWhy are rivers and lakes different? Applies L2’s hazard-reading to water with no flags, no patrol and no visibility.
5. Telling other peopleHow do I get this to someone who needs it? Requires all four: the campaign has to carry accurate content from each lesson.

Say the dependency aloud each lesson — “you cannot do today without last lesson, and here is why.” That sentence is the organiser doing its work.

3.2 Checks for understanding — the repertoireMONITOR PROGRESS · P1

Every lesson in this unit ends with an exit ticket. That is one technique at one moment. VTLM 2.0 asks for frequent checks during explanation and practice, with every student responding rather than volunteers. Use these inside the I do and we do phases.

TechniqueUse it here What it tells you
Hand signals “Thumb up if it is safe to swim here, down if it is not, sideways if you need more information” — over four beach photographs in L2. Whole-class read in five seconds. Sideways thumbs are the useful ones; ask one of them what information they want.
Mini-whiteboards “Write the next letter of DRS ABCD” — six quick rounds in L3, all boards up at once. Shows you who is sequencing and who is guessing from the person beside them.
Cold call After the rip explanation in L2: “Mia, what are you swimming towards, and why not the beach?” Name first, question second, no hands. Keeps every student ready to answer, not just the three who always volunteer.
Hinge question Mid-L3, before practice: “You find someone face-down in a pool. You are alone. First action: (a) start compressions (b) call 000 (c) check for danger (d) pull them out.” A deliberate fork. If more than a few pick (a) or (d), re-teach Danger before any practice begins.
Turn-and-talk Already used in L1 and L4. Follow it with a cold call on what their partner said. Makes the pair talk accountable instead of social.
Exit ticket In place at the end of all five lessons. Retrospective. Good for planning the next lesson, too late to change this one.

The point of the first five is that they happen while you can still change what you do. Choose two per lesson and plan where they land, rather than improvising.

Peer review needs a frame. This unit asks students to peer review in several places — the campaign, the debate, the portfolio. Unframed peer review produces “good job, maybe add more colour.” Give students three slots and require all three:

SlotSentence stem Applied to the Lesson 5 campaign
Kind“The strongest part is… because…” Forces a reason, so it cannot be “it looks good.”
Specific“In the [name the part], I noticed…” Must point at one element — the headline, the statistic, the image choice.
Helpful“One change that would meet criterion [X] is…” Ties the suggestion to a success criterion, not to personal taste.

Then leave class time for the author to act on it. Feedback students have no time to use is feedback that teaches them feedback does not matter.

3.3 Think-aloud scriptsEXPLICIT EXPLANATION AND MODELLING · P2

Demonstrating a skill shows students the actions. Thinking aloud shows them the decisions — which is the part they have to reproduce when no teacher is present. Use these close to verbatim the first time.

Lesson 2 · reading a beach photograph “Right — before I decide anything, I am looking for the flags. I can see red and yellow on the left, so that is where the patrol wants me. Now I am looking at the water between them … it is choppy, breaking white. Good. Now I look right, past the flags, and that is where I slow down: that patch is darker and it looks calmer. My instinct says calm means safe, and that instinct is exactly wrong here — calm, dark and no breaking waves is water moving out. I can also see a bit of foam trailing seaward. So: I swim on the left, between the flags, and I would not put a toe in that darker channel. Notice what I did — I did not look for danger first, I looked for the flags first, then checked my own instinct against what the water was doing.”
Lesson 3 · deciding on a rescue “Someone is in trouble ten metres off the bank. I can swim — I am a good swimmer, and that is the thought I have to argue with, because the statistics are full of good swimmers. So I run the order. Danger first: is it safe for me? No — that is moving water and I would be in it with a panicking person. So I am not going in. What can I reach them with? I am looking around — there is a branch, there is my jumper, there is an esky lid. The esky lid floats and I can throw it. So: I call out to them to float, I throw the lid, I phone 000, and I stay on the bank talking to them. I have done more for them from here than I would have done as a second person in the water.”

Both scripts deliberately name a wrong instinct and then argue with it. That is the move worth copying: students hold the misconception, so model overriding it rather than pretending it is absent.

3.4 Examples and non-examplesEXPLICIT EXPLANATION AND MODELLING · P2

Water safety is full of concepts students half-hold. A non-example marks the boundary of a concept and pre-empts the misconception. Present them in pairs and ask what changed.

ConceptExample Non-exampleThe misconception it heads off
Patrolled location A surf beach between the red-and-yellow flags, 1pm on a January Sunday. The same beach, same flags still in the sand, 7pm after the patrol has gone. That “patrolled” describes the place rather than the hours.
Rip current A darker, calmer channel with foam trailing seaward and no breaking waves. A wide area of white, broken, choppy water. That calm water is safe water.
A rescue you can attempt Throwing a boogie board from the bank and talking the person into floating. Swimming out to tow a panicking person back, because you made the swim team. That being a strong swimmer makes a swim rescue safe.
What drowning looks like Silent, upright, mouth at water level, no arm waving, under in 20–60 seconds. Shouting for help and waving both arms above the head. The film version — which is why bystanders miss it happening metres away.
Supervising a child An adult within arm’s reach, eyes on the child, phone down. An adult in the same backyard, reading, while the child is in the pool. That being nearby is the same as supervising. This one matters: a quarter of Victoria’s 2024–25 drowning deaths were in home environments.
Cold water shock Jumping into a 13°C Victorian river in October — involuntary gasp, then hyperventilation, within the first minute. A cold shower, which is unpleasant but does not take breathing control away. That cold water is a comfort problem rather than a breathing problem.
Wearing a life jacket Correct size, buckled, crotch strap done up, worn before leaving the ramp. Two jackets stowed under the seat of the boat, as the law requires them to be carried. That carrying one is being protected by one.

After each pair, ask the question that does the teaching: “What exactly changed between these two?” Students who can answer that hold the concept; students who cannot are recognising pictures.

3.5 Scaffolds, and when to take them awaySCAFFOLD PRACTICE · P1–P3

This unit is well supplied with scaffolds — word banks, sentence starters, scaffolded and simplified workbooks, answer keys. What it has not previously stated is when to withdraw them. A scaffold left in place past proficiency stops being support and starts being a ceiling.

ScaffoldFade it when the student… Fade it to…
Sentence starters in the written responses completes two responses using the starter without re-reading it. a bare prompt word, then nothing.
Word bank for the glossary terms uses three of the terms unprompted in discussion. a bank with the terms removed but the definitions left, then self-recall.
Hazard table with categories pre-filled adds a hazard you did not list. an empty table with the column headings only.
DRS ABCD prompt card during role-play runs the sequence twice without glancing at the card. card face down on the mat, available but not in view, then away.
Scaffolded workbook version finishes a scaffolded section early and accurately. the standard version for the next section, not the next unit.

Fade per student and per task, not per class. The scaffolded workbook is a response to a current need, not a label — a student can be on the scaffolded version for the data section and the standard version for the rescue section in the same lesson.

3.6 Review scheduleREVISIT AND REVIEW · P1–P2 · VARY PRACTICE · P2

The retrieval starters in Lessons 2–5 are daily review. VTLM 2.0 also asks for weekly and monthly routines, because the drop in retention is steepest soon after learning. This unit has an advantage most units do not: there is a real external deadline. The knowledge has to be available in January, not in the week of the assessment.

IntervalWhen What to retrieve, and how
DailyStart of Lessons 2–5 Three or four questions from prior lessons, no notes, self-marked, ungraded. Already built in.
WeeklyEnd of the unit week Brain dump: five minutes, blank page, everything you can recall about keeping yourself alive in water. Then compare against the glossary and add what was missed in a different colour. The gaps are the review list.
MonthlyOne starter in each of the next two HPE units Four interleaved questions mixing water safety with the current unit’s content, so students have to work out which knowledge applies before they answer. Interleaving is the point — do not label which question is which.
Pre-summerFinal HPE lesson before the summer holidays Fifteen minutes. Scenario cards, cold-called, no notes: a river in January, a backyard pool with a toddler, an unpatrolled beach at dusk. Hand out How to Revise Water Safety at the start of this session — its 20 no-notes questions are built for it. This is the retrieval that matters — Victorian drowning deaths peak over summer, and this is the last time you will have these students before then.

3.7 Group work protocolsAPPLY LEARNING AND BUILD MASTERY · P2

The Lesson 5 communication campaign and the Year 9–10 community action project are both group tasks. VTLM 2.0 asks that group work protocols be explicitly taught, not assumed, and that participation be accountable. Teach this once, in four minutes, before the campaign begins.

RoleDoes Is accountable for
Evidence keeper Finds and records every statistic used, with its source and year. Reporting to the group if a figure cannot be sourced — and the group dropping it.
Audience advocate Asks, at each decision, whether the chosen audience would actually read this. Naming the audience in one sentence at the start and holding the group to it.
Message editor Cuts the wording down and checks it against the success criteria. The final text being shorter than the first draft.
Designer Decides format, layout and how the message is seen. The format matching where the audience is, not personal preference.

Two rules, stated before groups form. One: the task is deliberately too large for one student, so a group that splits into one worker and three spectators will not finish — that is the design, not an accident. Two: each student writes one line at the end naming what they personally contributed, and the group signs off on it. That line is what makes the roles real.

3.8 Metacognitive promptsSELF-REGULATION · P2 · APPLY LEARNING · P2

VTLM 2.0 asks that students be taught to plan, monitor and evaluate their own learning, and that teachers model self-questioning aloud. Four prompts, used in this order, cover the cycle. They are worth putting on the wall for the duration of the unit.

StagePrompt Where it lands in this unit
Comprehension“What is this situation actually asking of me?” Scenario cards. Most wrong answers are students solving a different scenario.
Connection“Have I seen something like this before?” Lesson 4’s inland hazards — the reading-the-water skill transfers from Lesson 2.
Strategy“What did I use last time, and does it apply here?” Rescue decisions. The DRS order is the strategy; the question is whether they reach for it.
Reflection“Was that the best choice? What would I change?” After the role-play check and after the campaign self-assessment.

Model them on yourself first. In the Lesson 3 think-aloud above, “I am a good swimmer, and that is the thought I have to argue with” is a strategy prompt being used out loud. Students copy what they hear you do, not what they read on a wall.

3.9 A named process for analysis tasks — Read, Risk, Respond, Review APPLY LEARNING AND BUILD MASTERY · P2

This unit asks students to analyse scenarios, case studies and hazard maps repeatedly, and until now it never told them how. “Analyse this scenario” is an instruction to produce an output, not a method for producing it, and students without a method default to narrating what happened. Four steps, taught once and then named every time:

StepThe question students ask themselves What it stops them doing
Read What is actually happening here, and what do I already know that fits? Answering a scenario they have half-invented. Most wrong answers are a correct response to a different situation.
Risk What is the hazard, who is exposed to it, and what makes it dangerous? Jumping to a response before naming what the danger actually is.
Respond What are my options, and which is safest for me and for them? Offering the first idea. “Safest for me as well” is the clause that rules out the swim rescue.
Review Would that have worked? What would I change? Treating the first answer as the final one.
Say this out loud, or it will cause confusion. This unit now contains two four-part frameworks and students will conflate them. DRS ABCD is what you do in the water, in an emergency, in that order. Read–Risk–Respond–Review is how you think about a situation on paper, in class, when there is time. One is an action sequence; the other is an analysis method. Teach the distinction explicitly the first time you use the second one, and do not introduce them in the same lesson.

Model it on one scenario card with a think-aloud, then name the steps each time students use it. The point is that they can say which step they are on — a process students cannot name is not a process they can transfer.

Where this sits. These are techniques, not a separate programme — each one is used inside the lesson plans that follow. The strategy-by-strategy audit of the whole suite, including what it still does not do, is in the VTLM 2.0 Alignment Guide.

4. Lesson Plans
L1

Victoria's Drowning Crisis — Why It Matters to You

⏱ 50 min 📖 Workbook Sections 1 🎯 VC2HP8P10, VC2HP8P09 🏫 Classroom
🎯 FOCUS THE LEARNING 10 min

Learning objectiveUnderstand who drowns in Victoria, where it happens and why, and explain why 15–24-year-olds are over-represented.

    Success criteria — share these with students
  • I can state how many people drowned in Victoria in the most recent reporting year and name the two most common locations.
  • I can describe at least three reasons why 15–24-year-olds are over-represented in the statistics.
  • I can explain the difference between a number of deaths and a rate per 100,000 people, and say why the rate matters when comparing groups.

Opening Hook — "Could This Be You?"

  • Begin with the question on the board: "Name the THREE closest bodies of water to where you live."
  • Brief think-pair-share (2 min), then gather responses. Map on the whiteboard if possible.
  • Show the statistic: "In 2024–25, 52 people drowned in Victoria. There were no deaths in public swimming pools, the first time since 2019–20, but 25% happened in home environments. Seven were aged 15–24 — about 1 in 8, at a rate 21% above the ten-year average."
  • Ask: "What does this make you feel? Were you surprised? Why or why not?"
📣 EXPLICIT TEACHING — I DO 15 min

Teacher-Led Discussion: Why Teenagers Are at Risk

  • Walk through the key risk factors using Poster 3 as a visual reference (display on projector or print A3).
  • Discuss: peer pressure, risk-taking during adolescence, gender and risk-taking behaviour, cultural background, overconfidence.
  • Key teacher talking point: "The reason this unit is important is not because any of you are irresponsible — it's because the research shows that the circumstances leading to drowning often come up quickly, in social settings, exactly like where you go in summer."
🤝 GUIDED PRACTICE — WE DO 15 min

Data Investigation

  • Students complete Workbook Activity 1.1 (personal connection survey) independently — 5 min.
  • Students examine the data table in Activity 1.2 and complete the 'What does this tell us?' column — 8 min.
  • If internet available: students visit https://lsv.com.au/what-we-do/research-advocacy to cross-reference data.
✏️ INDEPENDENT APPLICATION — YOU DO 8 min

Written Responses (Questions 1.2a and 1.2b)

  • Students complete Questions 1.2a and 1.2b in their workbooks independently.
  • Circulate to check understanding. Prompt students who are struggling with: "Look at the data about WHERE drownings happen. What does that tell you?"
📝 MONITOR PROGRESS 5 min

Exit Ticket

  • On a sticky note or scrap of paper: "Write ONE fact from today that surprised you and ONE question you still have."
  • Collect at the door. Use questions to guide Lesson 2 content.
Review the success criteria. Return to the three success criteria on the board. Ask students to self-rate each one with a thumb (up / sideways / down). Any criterion with a spread of sideways and down thumbs is the one to re-teach at the start of Lesson 2.
📌 Pedagogical Notes
  • Avoid dramatising drowning deaths. The goal is empowerment through information, not fear. Keep a calm, factual tone.
  • Be aware that some students may have had personal experience with drowning-related loss. Brief students at the start that if anything feels too personal, they can step out or talk to you privately.
  • The gender discussion (males are 4× more likely to drown) is a strong discussion point — connect to broader HPE themes around gender norms, risk-taking, and social pressure. Handle sensitively.

⚠️ Pastoral Care Note: If any student discloses distress related to a drowning incident in their family or community, follow your school's wellbeing support protocols immediately. Before teaching this unit, read the cultural safety protocol — advance notice, opt-outs, First Nations protocols around deceased persons, and what to do if a student discloses. Have wellbeing referral information available before running this lesson.

L2

Beach Flags, Signals & Rip Current Survival

⏱ 50 min 📖 Workbook Section 2 🎯 VC2HP8P10 🏫 Classroom
🔁 REVIEW — RETRIEVAL PRACTICE 3 min

Retrieval starter — Lesson 1

  • Three questions on the board, answered individually in books with no notes, then checked as a class (2 min). 1. How many people drowned in Victoria in 2024–25? 2. What share of those deaths happened in home environments? 3. Name one reason 15–24-year-olds are over-represented.
Why this is here. Retrieving prior learning from memory, rather than re-reading it, is what moves it into long-term memory. Keep it low-stakes and ungraded — the point is the act of recall, not the score. VTLM 2.0: Retention and recall.
🎯 FOCUS THE LEARNING 8 min

Learning objectiveInterpret beach safety signage, identify a rip current, and explain how to survive one.

    Success criteria — share these with students
  • I can explain what the red and yellow flags mean and say exactly where it is safe to swim.
  • I can identify a rip current in a photograph and name two visual signs I used.
  • I can describe the three steps to take if caught in a rip, and explain why swimming straight for shore fails.

Flag Quiz — Prior Knowledge Probe

📌 RLSSA Throw Rescue Hierarchy

The Royal Life Saving Society Australia (RLSSA) recommend this rescue priority order: (1) Talk — shout instructions from safety; (2) Reach — extend something from the bank (pole, towel, clothing); (3) Throw — throw a buoyant object with a line; (4) Row — use a vessel if available; (5) Go — enter the water only if trained, never alone, always with a flotation device. Workbook Activity 5.2 asks students to apply this hierarchy. Source: https://www.royallifesaving.com.au

🦴 Activity 5.4 — Diving, Shallow Water & Spinal Injury (new)

This activity was added to address a significant gap in the original unit — diving and shallow-water spinal injury is the primary catastrophic risk for students in the 10–14 age group, yet it receives far less classroom attention than drowning. The three 2024–25 Australian case studies make this immediately relevant.

Key data to know before teaching:

  • Unsafe diving = 10% of all spinal cord injuries and 20% of quadriplegia cases in Australia (AIHW)
  • 95% of aquatic spinal injury patients are male — highest incidence age group: 10–14 years
  • Cervical vertebrae (C4–C6) fracture on head-first impact — spinal cord neurons cannot regenerate — two-thirds suffer permanent disability
  • Three 2024–25 case studies: Terrigal Wharf NSW (shallow tidal water beneath wharf), Torquay Front Beach VIC (shifting sandbank at surf beach), Bawley Beach NSW (sandbank formed between visits — false confidence)

Teaching approach:

  • Cold-open: display the six-stat data block (10%, 20%, 95%, 66%, 10–14, +34%) without context. Ask: "What do you think these numbers describe?" — consistently produces strong engagement because students are surprised it is not about drowning.
  • Read the three case studies as a class. For each, ask: "What specifically changed between the last safe visit and this injury?" — lead students to the mechanism (sandbank, tidal variation, wharf depth) rather than just "they made a bad decision."
  • Q5.4a asks students to reflect on their own risk — particularly for male students aged 10–14. Frame this as self-awareness, not accusation: "The data describes a pattern — your job is to understand whether it applies to you and why."
  • Sensitivity note: some students may have personal connections to these injuries. Brief the class that the content involves real injuries before showing the statistics.
  • Sources to cite: AIHW (aihw.gov.au), Royal Perth Hospital spinal injury review, RLSSA (royallifesaving.com.au)

    5. Student Workbook — Answer Key

    🔑 Section 1: Victorian Drowning Statistics

    ACTIVITY 1.2 — Data Table (What does this tell us?) interpretive — accept variety
    52 deaths: Victoria recorded 52 fatal drownings in 2024–25, below the 2020–21 peak of 64 and the 59 recorded in 2022–23, but 9% above the ten-year average of 48. A fall from a recent peak is not the same as the problem being solved.
    About 1 in 8 aged 15–24: Young people are significantly overrepresented, meaning age alone isn't protective — young people drown too.
    38 of 52 male (73%): Males take more physical risks near water, often influenced by peer pressure or overconfidence in their abilities.
    All at unpatrolled sites: If everyone swam only at patrolled locations, the death toll could theoretically be near zero.
    Q 1.2a — TWO reasons teenagers are at higher risk (2 marks) 1 mark per valid reason
    Accept any two of: risk-taking behaviour common in adolescence; peer pressure to swim in unsafe places; overconfidence in swimming ability; tendency to swim at unpatrolled locations; lack of awareness of hidden dangers like rip currents or cold water; social norms around not 'backing down' from dares.
    Q 1.2b — What unpatrolled location data suggests (2 marks)
    Model answer: Victoria recorded zero public-pool drowning deaths in 2024–25, which suggests trained lifeguards and supervision are extremely effective where they are present. The limit of that argument is that a quarter of deaths occurred in home environments, which no lifeguard can reach. Swimming at patrolled beaches/pools dramatically reduces risk because someone is watching and able to respond in seconds. This makes swimming at patrolled locations one of the single most important things people can do to stay safe.

    🔑 Section 2: Beach Flags & Rip Currents

    Activity 2.1 — Flag Identification
    Flag 1: Red & Yellow — Patrolled swimming area; always swim here
    Flag 2: Red — Dangerous conditions; experienced swimmers only or do not enter
    Flag 3: Black & White Quartered — Surfboards/watercraft zone; swimmers stay out
    Flag 4: Yellow + Black Ball — No surfboards; swimming only in this zone
    Flag 5: Orange Windsock — Offshore winds; no inflatables allowed
    Flag 6: Purple — Dangerous marine life spotted; swim with caution
    Q 2.2a — THREE steps if caught in a rip (3 marks, 1 per valid step)
    1. Stay calm and don't panic — conserve energy.
    2. Float or tread water rather than fighting the current.
    3. Swim parallel to the shore (not directly in) to escape the current's channel, then angle back to shore. If unable to reach shore, raise one arm and call/signal for help.
    Q 2.2b — Why swimming directly against a rip is dangerous (2 marks)
    A rip current can move at speeds up to 2.5 m/s — faster than most swimmers can sprint. Swimming directly against it uses enormous energy very quickly, leading to exhaustion and then drowning. The swimmer makes no progress against the current, becomes panicked, and loses the ability to stay afloat.
    Q 2.2c — Liam's scenario: TWO mistakes and what should have been done (4 marks)
    Mistake 1: Swimming at an unpatrolled beach. They should have gone to a patrolled beach where lifeguards could respond within seconds.
    Mistake 2: Liam tried to swim directly against the rip current, exhausting himself. He should have stayed calm, floated, swum parallel to shore, and if needed, raised his arm to signal for help.
    Bonus: His friends didn't know what to do — they should have known to call 000 immediately and throw something that floats rather than entering the water themselves.

    🔑 Section 3: DRS ABCD

    Activity 3.1 — DRS ABCD words
    D — DANGER | R — RESPONSE | S — SEND (for help / call 000) | A — AIRWAY | B — BREATHING | C — CPR | D — DEFIBRILLATION
    Q 3.2a — First action at River Rope Swing (2 marks)
    First action: Do NOT jump in. Call 000 (or shout for someone else to call while you act). Then use the rope from the swing to throw to the person (throw rescue). This is safer than entering water and risking becoming another casualty. The rope from the swing is already available.
    Q 3.2b — Why the friend jumping in was dangerous (2 marks)
    The untrained friend who jumped in creates a second casualty risk. When a person is drowning, they involuntarily grab anything within reach (including the rescuer) and may push the rescuer underwater in their panic. Without rescue training, entering the water dramatically increases the chance of two drownings, not one.

    🔑 Section 4: SwimSafe Rules, Hazard Mapping & Decision-Making

    Activity 4.0 — Hazard Mapping Table accept well-reasoned responses
    Beach (patrolled): Rip currents (H), sunburn/heat illness (M), jellyfish (M), rough surf/waves (H), swimming outside flags (H). Safe: swim between flags, check conditions, sun protection.
    River/Dam: Cold water shock (H), submerged objects/debris (H), strong currents especially after rain (H), no lifeguard (H), unknown depth (H). Safe: enter feet first, never swim alone, check weather/conditions, wear lifejacket for boating.
    Pool: Unsupervised young children (H), diving into shallow end (H), drains (M), glass near pool (L), no adult supervision (H). Safe: always watch children, no diving in shallow water, pool fencing.
    Q 4.0a — Most dangerous environment + evidence (2 marks)
    Model answer: Rivers and dams are the most dangerous overall. The LSV 2024–25 data shows 29% of deaths occurred in inland waterways (11 at rivers, creeks and streams) and 38% in coastal waterways, while public swimming pools recorded none. Unlike patrolled beaches, rivers have no lifeguard supervision, cold water shock risk, hidden currents, submerged hazards, and conditions that change rapidly (especially after rain). Accept any well-argued response supported by data.
    Activity 4 — LSV SwimSafe 5 Rules (from memory)
    1. Read signs · 2. Enter feet first · 3. Stay in depth · 4. Swim with a friend · 5. Signal for help
    Source: Life Saving Victoria — https://lsv.com.au

    🔑 Section 5: Inland Waterways, Throw Rescue & Alcohol

    Activity 5.2 — Throw Rescue Ranking (RLSSA hierarchy) accept logical reasoning
    Best to worst: 1. Life ring attached to jetty — attached, won't sink, can be pulled back; 2. Coiled rope/throw bag — can throw to distance and pull person in; 3. Extending towel/clothing from bank — limited reach, only effective if person is close; 4. Throwing pool noodle/esky lid — buoyant but no line to pull person in; 5. Jumping in — most dangerous option, should only be done by trained rescuers with a flotation device. Award marks for reasoning, not just ranking order.
    Source: RLSSA Talk → Reach → Throw → Row → Go hierarchy — https://www.royallifesaving.com.au
    Q 5.3a — THREE ways alcohol increases drowning risk (3 marks, 1 per valid effect)
    Accept any three of: impaired judgement (overestimates swimming ability, takes more risks); reduced coordination (can't swim effectively); slowed reaction time (slower to respond to danger); impaired body temperature regulation (increases hypothermia risk in cold water); reduced ability to hold breath; false confidence leading to swimming in unsafe locations or depths.
    Q 5.3b — TWO specific risks in pool party alcohol scenario (2 marks)
    Accept any two of: night-time swimming means poor visibility — harder to see if person goes under; alcohol impairs the ability to assess depth or distance; CPR/rescue response is complicated by the alcohol use of bystanders; a person who goes unconscious in the water at night may not be noticed; alcohol-affected friends are less likely to identify an emergency quickly and respond correctly. Accept well-reasoned alternatives.

    VTLM 2.0: Inclusive learning, Practice 1 — maintain high expectations, enable full participation. Departmental supports: the Diverse Learners Hub, Disability Inclusion funding, and your school’s wellbeing team for IEP processes.

    🌐 CALD (Culturally and Linguistically Diverse) Students

    Priority note: Life Saving Victoria reports that people from multicultural backgrounds accounted for 185 Victorian drowning deaths since 2015–16 — 37% of the total, with country of birth unknown in a further 22% of cases. This unit is particularly important for students, and the communication campaign task is the highest-impact activity for community reach.

    • LSV provides translated water safety resources in Arabic, Vietnamese, Chinese, Somali, and other languages: https://lsv.com.au/community/multicultural-safety/
    • The Victorian Government's water safety page also has multilingual resources: https://www.vic.gov.au/water-safety
    • Allow students to share culturally different relationships with water — not all cultures traditionally swim; validate diverse experiences while communicating the importance of learning water safety regardless of background.
    • Pair CALD students with supportive peers for scenario activities; allow translation of key terms using home language or digital tools where helpful.
    • Communication Campaign Option C (multilingual fact sheet) is specifically designed for CALD students — encourage them to create it in their home language. This is a highly meaningful task that extends reach into the community beyond the classroom.
    • When discussing fishing-related drowning (more than half of fishing deaths in the past decade involve people from CALD backgrounds), handle with cultural sensitivity — frame as information, not judgment.
    • If running Lesson 1 with a high-CALD cohort, be aware that some students may have direct family experience with water safety challenges related to cultural background and limited swimming opportunity.

    📈 Extension Students (High Achieving)

    • Assign Extension Scenario Cards X1, X2, and X3 from the Scenario Activity Card pack. X1 is a full data analysis task comparing Victorian and national drowning data; X2 is the communication campaign; X3 investigates the physiology of drowning (cerebral hypoxia, CPR mechanics).
    • Research task: Investigate how the LSV drowning report data compares to national data from Royal Life Saving Australia (https://www.royallifesaving.com.au/research). What are the key differences between Victorian and other state/territory rates?
    • Design a school swim safety awareness week proposal, including activities, Poster 4 (SwimSafe) display, guest speakers from LSV, and RLSSA resources.
    • Investigate the physiology of cold water shock using the 1-10-1 rule — write a summary for the school LMS or health notice board.
    • Compare dry-land water safety education approaches between Australia (LSV/RLSSA), UK (RLSS UK), and USA (Red Cross). What are the key differences? Which approach do you think is most effective for teenagers? Cite sources from all three organisations.
    • Gamification extension: Run all 12 scenario cards as a timed group challenge — groups compete to correctly answer the most scenarios. Teacher uses the on-screen answer reveals. Works well as an end-of-unit review or for a double lesson.

    ♿ Students with Additional Needs

    • Workbook activities can be completed verbally with a scribe or using speech-to-text tools.
    • CPR practical component: students with physical limitations can observe and provide verbal guidance to a partner — full marks available for verbal accuracy without physical demonstration.
    • Simplified answer scaffolds can be provided for written questions (sentence starters, word banks, visual cue cards).
    • For the Communication Campaign (Activity 6.2), the oral option — explaining to the teacher or presenting to a small group — is a valid and fully assessable alternative to written options.
    • The SwimSafe 5 rules (Poster 4) make a strong visual memory support — laminate a personal copy for students who benefit from visual cues.
    • Scenario Cards can be adapted — provide fewer questions per card, or pre-select the 5 most accessible cards for targeted use.

    🏊 Non-Swimmers and Students Who Avoid Water

    • This unit is deliberately fully classroom-based and dry-land. No student should feel embarrassed about swimming ability levels at any point.
    • Activity 1.1 (swimming ability self-rating) is for the student's personal reflection only — do not ask students to share this publicly.
    • Frame the unit message consistently: "Being water smart isn't about being a great swimmer — it's about making safe decisions before and after you get near water. The data shows that knowing what to do saves lives."
    • The SwimSafe rules and DRS ABCD are equally important for non-swimmers — in fact, recognising dangerous situations before entering water is arguably the most important skill for a non-swimmer to develop.
    • For students who have had negative water experiences, the Communication Campaign Option B (letter to younger student) allows them to process and share knowledge without requiring personal disclosure.
    🎓
    8. Teacher Background Knowledge

    🌊 Key Water Safety Facts for Teachers

    • The 1-10-1 rule (cold water): You have approximately 1 minute to control your breathing when you enter cold water, 10 minutes of useful swimming ability before your muscles lose coordination, and up to 1 hour of survival time before hypothermia sets in (in most adult adults).
    • Rip currents: Account for approximately 80% of lifeguard rescues at Australian beaches. They are NOT undertows (which don't exist) — they move laterally, not down. They are actually calmer-looking water between breaking waves.
    • CPR rates: Bystander CPR doubles or triples survival rates for cardiac arrest. Every second counts — CPR should start within 4 minutes of a cardiac arrest to give the best chance of brain function being preserved.
    • Throw, don't go: Most non-professional bystander drownings occur when a friend or family member attempts an in-water rescue without training. A throw rescue with any floating object from the bank is always safer.
    • AEDs: Automated External Defibrillators are in many public spaces in Victoria (pools, shopping centres, schools). They are designed to be used by untrained bystanders — the device will not shock a heart that doesn't need it. Know where your school's AED is located before teaching this unit.
    📌 Know Your School's Emergency Plan

    Before teaching this unit, ensure you know: (1) where your school's AED is located, (2) your school's emergency response protocol for water-related incidents at excursions, (3) the wellbeing referral pathway if a student shares personal trauma related to drowning. Review the DE Swimming and Water Safety policy annually.
